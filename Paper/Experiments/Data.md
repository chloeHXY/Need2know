## Data Construction

Existing privacy benchmarks for LLMs are predominantly organized around
*explicit personally identifiable information* (PII), e.g., names, emails,
addresses, and organization names [TO VERIFY: cite Pilán et al., 2022;
Lukas et al., 2023]. Yet in real interactions, users routinely disclose
information that is sensitive but *not identifying*—family relationships,
health conditions, financial pressure, whereabouts, third-party identities—
much of which is *unnecessary* for the task at hand. Following the
contextual-integrity view of privacy [TO VERIFY: cite Nissenbaum, 2010;
Mireshghallah et al., 2024; Ngong et al., 2025], we frame the rewriter's
target not as PII redaction but as the suppression of **task-non-essential
sensitive disclosures**, while preserving information that is *contextually
essential* to answering the user's request.

Concretely, every sample in our benchmark comes with two disjoint
attribute sets that directly instantiate the symbols used in
\S\ref{sec:reward}: a set of **essential** spans
$\mathcal{E}=\{e_i\}_{i=1}^{n}$ that must be preserved, and a set of
**non-essential** spans $\mathcal{N}=\{c_j\}_{j=1}^{K}$ that should be
removed or generalized. To populate $(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{N})$ with both
breadth and label reliability, we build two complementary subsets:
(i) a **general-domain** subset constructed via a reverse-synthesis
pipeline (\S\ref{sec:data:reverse}), and (ii) a **medical** challenge set
that stresses the over-removal failure mode in a domain where sensitive
information is often the answer itself (\S\ref{sec:data:medical}).
Detailed prompts, value banks, and per-domain pools are deferred to the
appendix.

### Reverse-Synthesis of General-Domain Samples
\label{sec:data:reverse}

We instead adopt a **reverse-synthesis**
design: we first sample a structured *semantic skeleton*—domain, task,
subject, and the partition of sensitive attributes into $\mathcal{E}$ and
$\mathcal{N}$—and only then ask a generator to embed this skeleton into a
fluent user prompt. An independent judge model re-extracts
$(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{N})$ from the surface text and is required to agree
with the sampler before a sample is admitted. This closed loop produces
labels that are *generated by construction* rather than inferred after
the fact.

**Structured sampler.** Following the taxonomy of Ngong et al.\ (2025) [TO
VERIFY], the sampler covers **11 privacy-relevant domains** (e.g., health,
finance, employment...), **20 task types** (e.g., summarization, translation, code
generation...), and **30 sensitive attribute types** (e.g.,family history, medication...). To inject a linguistically plausible prior over which attributes can be *essential*, we
attach to each domain a hand-curated *essential candidate pool* used as a **soft** prior—essential attributes are preferably, but not exclusively, drawn from this pool. To prevent stylistic collapse, we further sample three orthogonal axes: the *speaker subject* , the *embedding style*, and the *emotional tone*.

**Generator and consistency judge.** A generator LLM is conditioned on the
sampled skeleton and instructed to embed every non-essential attribute
naturally while making each essential attribute *semantically load-bearing*,
i.e., its removal should make the request unanswerable at the same
quality. To avoid co-adaptation between the generator and the labeler, we
deliberately use **heterogeneous models**: Claude Sonnet 4.6 [TO VERIFY:
cite] as the generator and GPT-4o [TO VERIFY: cite] as the judge. The
judge sees only the surface prompt and re-extracts a triple
(*primary context*, $\hat{\mathcal{E}}$, $\hat{\mathcal{N}}$). We then
match $\hat{\mathcal{E}}$ against the sampler's $\mathcal{E}$ (and likewise
for $\mathcal{N}$) using a two-stage matcher: first, a stop-word-filtered
**token-overlap** coverage with threshold $0.5$; if it fails, we fall back
to **embedding cosine similarity** with threshold $0.65$ to absorb
paraphrastic drift.Only samples whose extracted labels agree with the sampler at both ends
of the partition are kept. We provide the full prompts, thresholds, and
failure-mode statistics in the appendix.

### Medical Challenge Set
\label{sec:data:medical}

Medical queries are a particularly adversarial setting for a privacy
rewriter: clinical signals are themselves *sensitive*, yet they are precisely the information the downstream model needs to answer correctly. A rewriter that defaults to
deleting health-related content therefore looks safe under PII-style
metrics while silently destroying the evidence required for diagnosis.
To explicitly stress this **over-removal** failure mode, we build a
medical challenge set on top of board-exam-style multiple-choice items
[TO VERIFY: cite medical-o1].

**Essential extraction.** For each item we use its existing chain-of-thought
solution and verified answer to drive GPT-4o to **verbatim** extract the
contiguous spans $\mathcal{E}$ of the question stem that are decisive for the final
diagnosis or option choice.

**Controlled disclosure injection.** We then rewrite each item with a
strictly constrained instruction that *appends* synthetic, clinically
inert disclosures forming $\mathcal{N}$. The instruction enforces a
*white/grey/black-list* contract: removing $\mathcal{N}$ should never change the correct option, so any drop
in answer accuracy after rewriting can be attributed to the rewriter
having removed parts of $\mathcal{E}$.

### Summary and Use in Training and Evaluation

The two subsets share a single abstraction—each sample carries an explicit
$(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{N})$ partition consumed without modification by the
reward function in \S\ref{sec:reward}. The general-domain subset supplies
broad coverage of *task-driven*, *context-driven*, and *addressed-person*
disclosure patterns, while the medical subset supplies a worst-case test
of over-removal in a high-stakes domain. Statistics, prompt templates,
quality-control thresholds, and the engineering pipeline are reported in
the appendix.
